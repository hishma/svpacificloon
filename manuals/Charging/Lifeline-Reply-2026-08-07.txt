Hello,

Thank you for the detailed information. You have clearly spent quite a bit of
time working through the charging history and taking good measurements, and I
agree that chronic undercharging/sulfation is a reasonable concern based on what
you have described.

I would start by separating the two 12V series strings exactly as you are
proposing. At this point, testing them independently will tell us much more than
measurements taken while the two strings remain paralleled.

One clarification regarding the 6.66V / 6.02V readings: that voltage split
definitely indicates a significant imbalance within String B, but I would not
conclude from those readings alone that Position 4 has necessarily been held at
6.66V by current flowing from String A. Because Positions 3 and 4 are in series,
their individual voltages can divide unevenly while the total string voltage
remains the same. Isolating the string and observing the individual battery
voltages over time will be much more useful.

For String A, I would proceed as follows:

Isolate the two GPL-4CT batteries from the other string and all vessel loads.

Perform a normal full charge first at the proper temperature-compensated
Lifeline charging voltage.

Once fully charged, perform one 8-hour conditioning charge at the
temperature-corrected conditioning voltage.

At approximately 70F, your calculation of approximately 15.58V for the 12V
series pair is correct. Lifeline specifies an 8-hour conditioning charge after a
normal full charge when attempting to recover capacity lost from extended
partial-state-of-charge operation.

If your Magnum limits the conditioning/equalization function to four hours,
restarting the function to complete approximately eight total hours is
reasonable, provided the voltage remains properly temperature compensated and
the batteries are being monitored.

After conditioning, allow the batteries to cool/rest and then perform a capacity
test.

I would not perform multiple conditioning cycles back-to-back simply based on
voltage. Let the capacity test determine whether another cycle is justified. If
the first conditioning cycle produces a meaningful improvement but capacity is
still below where it needs to be, another conditioning/retest cycle can be
attempted. If repeated conditioning produces little or no improvement,
additional high-voltage charging is unlikely to restore useful capacity.

Also, one correction regarding the proposed 110Ah capacity test: the series pair
is still a 220Ah battery bank. Connecting two 220Ah 6V batteries in series
doubles the voltage but does not halve the Ah capacity.

Lifeline's preferred capacity test is a controlled constant-current discharge.
Our published procedure calls for bringing the battery fully charged and
temperature stabilized, then discharging at the specified test current to the
appropriate end voltage and comparing the discharge time against the battery's
published rating. Capacity below approximately 80% is considered degraded and is
a reason to attempt conditioning; below approximately 50% generally indicates
replacement should be considered.

Regarding String B, I would definitely test it before declaring either battery
scrap.

After separating the string, fully charge it if it can be charged normally,
disconnect it completely, and record the individual battery voltages after
approximately 24 hours. Continue recording them over several days.

A healthy Lifeline AGM has very low self-discharge, so a battery that shows a
substantial continuing voltage decline while completely isolated is much more
concerning than one that simply stabilizes at a low open-circuit voltage.

The 6.02V reading on Position 3 alone is not enough for me to diagnose an
internal short. Severe sulfation and imbalance can produce some unusual
individual battery voltages when batteries are connected in series. If Position
3 continues losing voltage rapidly after being fully charged and completely
disconnected, however, that would point us toward an internal problem rather
than ordinary sulfation.

Likewise, I would not automatically discard Position 4 because of the 6.66V
reading. The more important questions are whether it will accept a normal
charge, hold open-circuit voltage independently, and deliver useful capacity.

Regarding temperature during conditioning, there is always more heat generation
during an elevated-voltage conditioning charge, so ventilation and monitoring
are important. Lifeline's technical information identifies thermal runaway as a
potential VRLA hazard when charging voltage and/or battery temperature becomes
excessive and emphasizes proper voltage, temperature compensation, and
ventilation.

If a battery begins heating rapidly relative to the others, shows abnormal
swelling, vents heavily, develops an unusual odor, or its temperature continues
climbing rather than stabilizing, discontinue the conditioning charge and
investigate before continuing. Our technical procedures also use 130F / 55C as a
stop point during certain recovery charging procedures, so I would certainly not
allow a battery to approach that temperature.

Running String A alone as a 12V, 220Ah house bank is perfectly acceptable
electrically provided the batteries prove serviceable through testing. You will
simply have half of the original bank capacity, so your depth of discharge will
increase substantially unless your consumption is reduced accordingly.

Your approximately 100A charger is also not excessive for a 220Ah Lifeline bank.
Lifeline AGMs have very high charge acceptance, and adequate charging current is
beneficial in deep-cycle applications.

Regarding the Magnum's 180-minute absorption limitation, I would not replace an
otherwise functional charger solely because it cannot provide four hours
automatically. The 2-, 3-, and 4-hour absorption recommendations correspond to
increasing depth of discharge, but the more meaningful indication of full charge
is acceptance current. Lifeline considers the battery fully charged when charge
current has tapered to approximately 0.5A per 100Ah of rated capacity at the
proper absorption voltage.

For your original 440Ah bank, that target is approximately 2.2A. For a single
220Ah string, it would be approximately 1.1A.

If your Magnum reaches that acceptance level before the timer expires, there is
no advantage in forcing the battery to remain in absorption merely to satisfy a
clock. Conversely, if it reaches its 180-minute limit while the batteries are
still accepting substantially more than the target current, the bank has not
completed the recharge.

The very short bulk period you observed after drawing the bank to 12.5V is
another reason I would separate the strings before drawing conclusions. With the
imbalance presently visible, the charger can reach its voltage threshold because
one battery or one string rises rapidly even though the bank as a whole has
accepted very little energy.

For routine conditioning after you establish what capacity remains, I would base
the interval primarily on how the boat is actually used. Lifeline's current
guidance describes approximately four hours every three to four weeks for
applications that routinely cannot achieve complete recharge.

Given the history you described, I would concentrate first on recovering and
testing the bank rather than immediately establishing a recurring conditioning
schedule. Once you know what String A and String B actually deliver
independently, you can make a much better decision about whether either string
is worth keeping in service and how aggressive the maintenance routine needs to
be.
